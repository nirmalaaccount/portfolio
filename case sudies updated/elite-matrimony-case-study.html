Designing for the client who will never fill in a form casually

Elite Matrimony is Matrimony.com's private matchmaking practice for India's wealthiest families. I redesigned its landing page around one problem the live site hadn't solved: it applied mass-market matrimony conventions to a service whose entire value is exclusivity and restraint.

Problem

A private, invitation-only matchmaking service used mass-market conversion tactics — 9 pricing points, 5 video carousels, 13 truncated testimonials — that undercut the exclusivity it was selling.

Context

A service that sells the opposite of a platform

Elite Matrimony sits inside Matrimony.com, the publicly listed company behind BharatMatrimony. It has operated since 2008, serving over 100,000 members through more than 100 dedicated relationship managers. Memberships are tiered by net worth — Elite Professional, Elite Business (up to ₹200 crore net worth), and Elite Aristocrat (above ₹200 crore) — each with duration-based pricing from ₹50,000 to ₹1,50,000.

This is not a browse-and-swipe matrimony platform. A relationship manager holds each client's brief personally, searches a closed register, and makes introductions one family at a time. That creates an unusual design brief: every convention that makes a typical matrimony product feel trustworthy — visible scale, testimonial walls, feature-dense pricing — is precisely what a discretion-first, relationship-led service has to avoid signalling.

The audit

What I actually reviewed, and what I found

I ran a structured heuristic audit of the live site — elitematrimony.com — rather than starting from assumption. Every finding below is verifiable by visiting the page directly.

Verified All findings in this section were re-checked against the live site on the day this case study was written — not carried over from an earlier note.

What I found

  • The enquiry form sits inside the hero, above any explanation of the service — a visitor is asked for name, alliance preference, mother tongue, and mobile number before they've been told why they should trust the organisation with that information.
  • A five-video TV commercial carousel, each a regional-language variant of the same ad, sitting between the value proposition and the "How it works" explanation.
  • Thirteen success stories, each truncated at roughly 60 words with a "Read more" link — high scroll cost for low signal per story.
  • A three-tier × three-duration pricing structure (Professional / Business / Aristocrat, each at 3 months / 6 months / "Till U Marry") — up to nine price points a visitor has to parse before knowing which applies to them.
  • Eight FAQ items doing explanatory work — including "Is Elite Matrimony different from regular matrimonial sites?" — that the page body should have already answered by that point.
  • No qualification signal above the fold. A ₹300 crore family and an unqualified visitor see the identical hero and the identical form.

Why it matters commercially

For a service run by 100+ relationship managers, the page has one real job: send qualified enquiries to a human. Volume is actively costly here — every unqualified enquiry consumes relationship-manager time that could go to a ₹1,50,000 member.

Design goal I set

Increase the proportion of enquiries that are actually qualified, while establishing enough trust in the organisation's discretion that a prospect is willing to hand over sensitive information at all.

These two goals pull against each other — filtering people out vs. inviting them in. Most decisions below are attempts to hold both at once.

What this audit did not include

I did not conduct relationship-manager interviews or an analytics review for this redesign — this is a heuristic and competitive audit, not a mixed-methods research study. I'm stating that plainly rather than implying a research depth that isn't there.

Why sequence is the real design decision

The content on both pages is nearly identical — the same process, the same testimonials, the same pricing. The difference is entirely the order. Moving the form from position 1 to position 6, and putting privacy before pricing, is an IA decision, not a visual one. That's what this project is really about.

Early directions

Two approaches I considered and moved away from

Before settling on a sequenced trust narrative, I worked through two other directions. Both were plausible on paper — here's why I stepped back from each.

Direction A — not used

Lead with a "Who we serve" filter

The first idea was to open the page with an explicit qualifier — net worth thresholds, occupation categories — so the right visitor self-identifies before seeing anything else. Seen on some ultra-premium services.

Why I moved away from it

Putting financial thresholds at the very top reads as a bouncer, not an invitation. For a service that emphasises relationship and discretion, the first impression should feel like you're being welcomed into something exclusive — not screened at a velvet rope. The qualification signal is still in the hero, but it's framed as "who this is designed for" rather than "prove you qualify."

Direction B — not used

Remove pricing from the page entirely

Some luxury services deliberately hide pricing — "enquire for details" — to preserve mystique and gate the conversation behind a phone call. I considered this seriously for Elite Matrimony.

Why I moved away from it

Elite Matrimony already lists pricing publicly on the live site. Hiding it in the redesign would create an inconsistency with the live product, and for a parent researching in private at 11pm, "call us for pricing" adds friction at exactly the wrong moment. Pricing stays — but sequenced after trust has been built, not before.

The bridge

The decisions that got from before to after

1

Sequence around the buyer's real decision, not a generic funnel

Instead of an AIDA-style attention→interest→action structure, I ordered the page around the actual question a prospect works through: am I the right kind of client → can I trust this organisation with sensitive information → who exactly will contact me. That's why privacy and process now sit before pricing.

2

Collapse 9 price points into 3 tiers

The live site separates package tier from duration, forcing a mental cross-reference. I merged them into three self-contained cards (Elite, Royal Business, Aristocrat) so a visitor self-selects in one glance instead of comparing a matrix.

3

Replace video carousel with a 5-step process narrative

The TV commercials are marketing assets, not proof of process. I replaced that slot with a concrete 5-step journey (Member Briefing → Profile Discovery → Curated Matches → Introductions → Relationship Guidance) that shows what actually happens after enquiry — since this is a relationship-led service, not a self-serve one.

4

Cut story count, raise story completeness

13 truncated testimonials ask for effort (tap "Read more") for a small payoff. I cut to 3 stories but removed all truncation — full quotes, named couples, city and profession context — trading breadth for credibility per story.

Mobile

A parent reading on a phone, not a founder skimming a laptop

A meaningful share of enquiries for this service come from a parent rather than the prospective bride or groom — often 50–70, reading on a large-screen phone rather than scanning quickly. That shaped the mobile design directly: the couple photo and stats reflow above the text rather than being cropped out, trust badges stack instead of compressing, and the CTA pair stacks full-width instead of shrinking to fit a row.

Reflection

What this is, and what it isn't yet

This is a completed design deliverable, audited against the live site and ready for stakeholder review and dev handoff. It is not yet backed by conversion data, A/B results, or relationship-manager interviews — I'm not going to claim results I can't defend. If I were continuing this work, the next steps would be: (1) validating the qualification framing with a handful of real enquirers before publishing any net-worth threshold copy more prominently, and (2) instrumenting the new enquiry flow so qualified-lead rate — not just submission volume — can actually be measured post-launch.

Post-design cognitive walkthrough

After completing the redesign, I ran a cognitive walkthrough from three user perspectives — a parent researching on mobile, a self-made professional, and a first-time visitor. I identified 5 potential gaps: undefined pricing tier differentiators, no timeline indication in the process, unclear brand relationship for new visitors, success stories not covering the full user range, and form field ambiguity. Checking each against the actual Figma, all 5 were already addressed — tier benefits are listed per card, the FAQ answers "how long does it take" with a 6–12 month range, the footer carries the Matrimony.com group relationship, the stories include a tech founder and an NRI family, and the form was removed entirely in favour of a consultation CTA. Running the walkthrough after the fact confirmed the design held up — but it also showed me the value of doing it earlier in the process, before the design is called finished.

The hardest part wasn't making the page feel expensive. It was accepting that for this audience, restraint converts better than persuasion — and then removing things that looked impressive but did nothing for a family deciding whether to trust us with something private.
